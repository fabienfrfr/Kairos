\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background:} Small language models trained on a single local machine leave
little room for architectural trial and error. Kairos combines bidirectional gated-DeltaNet linear attention and sliding-window attention
over shared projections (LiZAttention2), block-wise Attention-Residual aggregation, a
sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) feed-forward layer and a multi-scale byte-level codec,
trained with a uniform-noise denoising objective over one byte space shared by text, image,
audio, video, lidar and control streams; this report asks whether the combination optimizes
at all before asking whether it is useful.
\textbf{Methods:} We give code-derived algorithms for each component, including a
cross-session recurrent-state memory gate, then run convergence sanity checks: overfitting
a single 440-byte sentence at 1--15M parameters, text only, with three seeds for codec and
attention choices, four seeds for the training objective and the training algorithm, and single-run sweeps for seven further factors.
\textbf{Results:} The two attention variants tested at 3\,000 steps (LiZAttention2 and
window attention alone) reach a minimum loss near $10^{-3}$. After 600 steps, the mean
minimum loss ranges from 0.017 (patch codec) to 0.273 (depthwise convolutional codec) with
the same codec ranking on every seed (five seeds, three for the two largest codecs). The
hybrid attention is not separable from window attention alone ($0.273\pm0.309$ against
$0.336\pm0.177$). An apparent advantage of shared over separate attention projections, seen in
unseeded single runs, closes with more steps or more diverse data, reverses when the projections are
frozen, and does not replicate across five seeded runs.
\textbf{Conclusions:} These checks suggest that the implementation optimizes; they say
nothing about generalization, multimodal training or comparison with existing models, which
a planned pretraining run is meant to address. The Discussion sizes a 200M-total/25M-active
target analytically; none of its throughput figures is measured.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Small, edge-deployable language models
(MobileLLM~\citep{liu2024mobilellm}, SmolLM2~\citep{allal2025smollm2},
Gemma~3~\citep{gemma3}) are routinely trained on far more tokens than the
Chinchilla-optimal ratio~\citep{hoffmann2022chinchilla}, on the premise that
inference cost dominates total cost of ownership. That premise says nothing
about training cost when the training hardware is itself constrained, the
setting of this report. Three architectural levers target efficiency in
largely separate literatures: (i) sub-quadratic sequence mixing, through
local windowed attention~\citep{beltagy2020longformer,jiang2023mistral} and
linear state-compressing mixers derived from state-space models
(Mamba~\citep{gu2023mamba}) and the delta
rule~\citep{yang2024deltanet,yang2024gateddeltanet}; (ii) sparse capacity
scaling through MoE
routing~\citep{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2021switch,dai2024deepseekmoe,deepseekv3};
(iii) non-autoregressive discrete diffusion
training~\citep{austin2021d3pm,lou2023sedd,sahoo2024mdlm,nie2025llada}, which
generalizes masked language modeling~\citep{devlin2018bert}. A fourth,
orthogonal choice removes the tokenizer: byte-level
inputs~\citep{xue2022byt5,yu2023megabyte,pagnoni2024blt} give one vocabulary
that can cover several modalities, an idea we had explored at smaller scale for two
modalities~\citep{furfaro2024pixelbytes} and revise substantially here for six.

Each lever is usually reported on its own. Combining them in one model small enough to
train on a single local machine raises a practical question before any question of
quality: does the combination optimize at all? Hybrid attention, sparse routing, a
multi-scale codec and a corruption-based objective each add moving parts that could
prevent optimization, and a memorization test is the cheapest way to check.
\textbf{Research question:} can a Kairos network drive the denoising loss of a fixed byte
string to near zero, and which design choices change how fast?

\textbf{In this paper}, we specify the Kairos architecture from its implementation
(Figure~\ref{fig:architecture_overview}) and report convergence sanity checks at
1--15M parameters. These checks are necessary and not sufficient: they do not measure
generalization. Held-out training and a comparison with Pythia-14M~\citep{biderman2023pythia}
are planned and not reported (\S\ref{sec:future}).

\textbf{Our contributions are:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The Kairos architecture (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture_overview}), specified from its implementation:
        the multimodal byte tokenizer (\S\ref{sec:tokenizer}), PyramidalCodec (Algorithm~\ref{alg:codec}), LiZAttention2
        (Algorithm~\ref{alg:liz}), Block-AttnRes
        (Algorithm~\ref{alg:attnres}), sparse MoE (\S\ref{sec:moe}), a
        cross-session memory gate (Algorithm~\ref{alg:memgate}) and the
        denoising objective with its curriculum
        (Algorithm~\ref{alg:diffusion}), each adapted from a specific prior
        method or, for the codec and the memory gate, combining existing
        ideas.
  \item A convergence study at tiny scale (\S\ref{sec:ablations}, \S\ref{sec:flowarms}) with seeded
        multi-seed statistics for codec, attention and training-objective choices and single-run sweeps for
        seven further factors.
  \item A worked correlation-versus-causation case
        (\S\ref{sec:qkv-sharing}): a clear single-run effect that does not
        survive three controls or a seeded replication, and a recommendation to report
        steps-to-threshold rather than loss at a fixed step.
\end{enumerate}
The Discussion also sizes an untrained 200M-total/25M-active target with a roofline argument
(\S\ref{sec:sizing}); it is analytical and not measured.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Efficient attention.} Full self-attention~\citep{vaswani2017attention}
is quadratic in sequence length. The delta
rule~\citep{yang2024deltanet,yang2024gateddeltanet} improves plain linear
attention with an error-correcting state update; Kairos runs it
\emph{bidirectionally} (a forward pass and a time-reversed pass,
concatenated) to match the context a denoising objective needs.
Sliding-window attention~\citep{beltagy2020longformer,jiang2023mistral} is
likewise bidirectional here, with rotary position
embeddings~\citep{su2021roformer}; the code supports grouped-query
attention~\citep{ainslie2023gqa}, which the configurations reported here do
not exercise. Coupling a delta-rule branch and a window-attention branch
through shared projections follows TPTT~\citep{furfaro2025tptt}.

\paragraph{Byte-level, hierarchical and multimodal models.} ByT5 removes the
subword tokenizer~\citep{xue2022byt5}; Kairos' tokenizer subclasses it and
extends it to other modalities. MegaByte models bytes with a global model over
fixed-size patches and a local model inside each
patch~\citep{yu2023megabyte}; the Byte Latent Transformer forms patches
dynamically from next-byte entropy~\citep{pagnoni2024blt}; Hourglass
Transformers shorten and re-expand the sequence inside one
stack~\citep{nawrot2022hourglass}; Perceiver~IO reads arbitrary input arrays,
including raw bytes, through cross-attention into a fixed latent
array~\citep{jaegle2022perceiverio}. Kairos' PyramidalCodec instead keeps
$S$ parallel views of one sequence at geometrically growing patch sizes,
runs a backbone on each, and fuses them after upsampling. Next-Concept
Modeling~\citep{kumbong2026ncm} also builds a multiscale hierarchy over text, with a residual
VQ-VAE producing discrete codes that a transformer generates one level at a time; the PyramidalCodec
is not quantized and processes its levels in parallel inside the backbone.

\paragraph{Sparse MoE.} MoE
architectures~\citep{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2021switch,dai2024deepseekmoe,deepseekv3}
decouple capacity from per-token compute. \texttt{KairosMoE} subclasses
DeepSeek-V3's routed-expert layer~\citep{deepseekv3} (sigmoid routing scores,
shared experts, and load balancing by a per-expert selection bias rather than
an auxiliary loss), and \texttt{KairosFFN}, used when MoE is disabled,
subclasses Qwen2's dense MLP~\citep{qwen2techreport}. Only the expert-weight
initialization is patched, because the upstream implementation used leaves
those weights uninitialized.

\paragraph{Cross-layer residual aggregation.} Block-AttnRes replaces the
fixed, unit-weight residual accumulation standard since
ResNet~\citep{he2015resnet} with the learned, block-windowed softmax
attention over earlier layer outputs of \citet{kimiteam2026attnres}, used
here as published (\S\ref{sec:attnres}).

\paragraph{Discrete diffusion language modeling.} Kairos' corruption is
closest to the uniform-replacement transition of D3PM~\citep{austin2021d3pm}:
corrupted positions are filled with tokens drawn uniformly from the
vocabulary, and the model is not told which positions were corrupted, unlike
the absorbing mask state of MDLM~\citep{sahoo2024mdlm}. The loss is weighted
by $1/p$ in the manner of MDLM, with a clip, and trained with
self-conditioning~\citep{chen2022analogbits} and a three-stage curriculum
(\S\ref{sec:objective}).

\paragraph{Flow matching for language.} The corruption above is also a discrete flow matching
path~\citep{gat2024dfm} with a uniform source and a linear scheduler, so the unweighted
objective ($\alpha=0$) is its posterior cross-entropy. Discrete flow matching was scaled to
1.7B parameters on code~\citep{gat2024dfm}; continuous flow-matching language models
now include ELF~\citep{hu2026elf} (105M to 652M parameters), LangFlow~\citep{chen2026langflow}
(130M) and FLM~\citep{lee2026flowmaplm}, all evaluated on OpenWebText. Kairos
does not train with any of them; the trainer exposes the discrete scheduler (\texttt{mask\_path}) and
an Euler sampler for it (\texttt{dfm\_sample}); both were compared with the current objective and sampler only on the overfitting protocol (\S\ref{sec:flowarms}).

\paragraph{Optimizers and noise sampling.} Muon~\citep{jordan2024muon} orthogonalizes the update of each 2D weight
matrix and was reported at about twice the compute efficiency of AdamW at multi-billion scale~\citep{liu2025muon};
block diffusion clips the noise schedule to reduce gradient variance~\citep{arriola2025bd3lm}. We test Muon on the
backbone matrices and stratified or antithetic draws of the noise level at tiny scale (\S\ref{sec:optarms}); we do not
test other optimizers.

\paragraph{Recurrent memory.} Transformer-XL carries hidden states across
segments~\citep{dai2019transformerxl}, Titans learn a test-time
memory~\citep{behrouz2025titans}, and TPTT retrofits such memory into
pretrained transformers~\citep{furfaro2025tptt}. Kairos' gate is simpler: it
seeds the recurrent state of each DeltaNet layer for a new sequence from a
learned blend of states left by earlier sequences (\S\ref{sec:memgate}).

\paragraph{Our positioning.} Kairos assembles components from the works above and adds
two elements: a pyramidal codec that runs the backbone on several patch scales, and a
cross-session memory gate. We did not perform a systematic literature search and claim no novelty beyond this combination. The aim of this report is to check that the
combination trains, not that it outperforms.

\section{Model}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture_overview} gives the data flow. The subsections follow it from
input bytes to output logits, then the training objective.

\subsection{Multimodal byte tokenizer}
\label{sec:tokenizer}

The tokenizer subclasses the ByT5 tokenizer~\citep{xue2022byt5}. Text is UTF-8 and every byte is
one id, with the same byte-to-id offset in all modalities, so one vocabulary of 290 entries (3 ByT5
special ids, 256 byte values, 31 added markers) serves every modality. A sequence is a list of
typed segments, each wrapped in modality delimiters (for example \texttt{<IMG>}\ldots\texttt{</IMG>})
and optionally in channel delimiters (R, G, B, left, right). Encoding returns three aligned
streams: the ids, a modality id per position, which drives the scale router of the codec, and an
\emph{octet-family} id per position, which names the byte plane a byte belongs to. Families live
in a separate stream rather than in the vocabulary, so one byte value can carry different meanings
in different planes (18 family ids, family 0 being text and markers).

Non-text values are serialized without a header (Table~\ref{tab:tokenizer}). A float
$x\in[\ell,u]$ becomes $n$ big-endian byte planes, as the digits of a place-value number (Eq.~\ref{eq:quant}):
\begin{equation}
q=\Big\lfloor \text{clip}\Big(\tfrac{x-\ell}{u-\ell},0,1\Big)\,(256^{n}-1)\Big\rfloor,\qquad
b_k=\Big\lfloor q/256^{\,n-1-k}\Big\rfloor \bmod 256,
\label{eq:quant}
\end{equation}
with $n=2$ by default and fixed ranges ($[-1,1]$ for audio and control signals, $\pm100$ for lidar
coordinates, $[0,1]$ for intensity), so the encoding needs no per-sample header. Optional
downsampling before quantization (block mean for pixels, decimation by 4 for audio and signals by
default, point stride for lidar) makes it lossy. Decoders recover shapes by counting markers and
raise an error on a malformed stream, so a generated stream can be checked.

\subsection{Embedding and PyramidalCodec}

Each token id and its octet-family id are embedded and fused by a linear layer (\S\ref{sec:tokenizer});
the output head, tied to the token embedding, predicts both the byte and its family.

The PyramidalCodec (Figure~\ref{fig:codec}) builds $S$ parallel views of the sequence with patch sizes
$p_s=\text{stride}^s$ (notebook config: stride $3$, $S=4$, so $p=3,9,27,81$). A shared
rank-$r$ bottleneck mixer
($\text{Conv1d}_{d\to r}\to\text{GELU}\to\text{Conv1d}_{r\to d}$,
$r=\max(8,d/4)$, cost $O(rd)$ against $O(d^2)$ for a dense $1{\times}1$
convolution) supplies cross-channel context once; each scale then applies a
grouped convolution with kernel and stride $p_s$ and its own mixer
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:codec}). The group count is the one knob that trades
compute for expressiveness: \texttt{channels\_per\_group}$=1$ (default) is
depthwise and cheapest, and raising it toward $d$ approaches a dense
convolution. \texttt{mode="patch"} replaces the convolution and mixer chain by
one $\text{Linear}(p_s d\to d)$ per scale, decoded with the transposed weight
(tied encode and decode). Each scale runs through its own backbone, or through
one shared backbone (about $75\%$ fewer backbone parameters for $S=4$). A
scale router lets each modality read only a subset of scales (notebook config: text
$\to$ scales 1--2, control 1, image 2--3, lidar 2, audio 3, video 3--4,
metadata 4); positions of modalities not routed to a scale bypass its
backbone. Decoding mirrors encoding with grouped transposed convolutions and
per-scale mixers, then concatenates the scales, applies RMSNorm and fuses them
with a linear layer.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{PyramidalCodec, \texttt{mode="conv"}: encode, per-scale backbone, decode}
\label{alg:codec}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times L\times d}$; patch sizes $p_1,\ldots,p_S$; backbones $f_1,\ldots,f_S$
\State $h\gets\text{Mixer}_{\text{pre}}(x)$ \Comment{shared channel mixing, computed once}
\For{$s=1$ \textbf{to} $S$}
  \State $h_s\gets\text{GroupedConv1d}\big(\text{PadToMultiple}(h,p_s);\ \text{kernel}=\text{stride}=p_s\big)$
  \State $z_s\gets\text{Mixer}_s(h_s)$ \Comment{$\lceil L/p_s\rceil$ positions}
  \State $\tilde z_s\gets f_s(z_s)$ \Comment{active positions only, chosen by the scale router}
  \State $u_s\gets\text{Trim}_L\big(\text{Mixer}'_s(\text{ConvTranspose1d}(\tilde z_s))\big)$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\text{Linear}_{Sd\to d}\big(\text{RMSNorm}(\text{concat}(u_1,\ldots,u_S))\big)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{LiZAttention2: shared-projection bidirectional hybrid attention}
\label{sec:liz}

Each layer runs a bidirectional sliding-window branch (mask $|q-k|\le w$, RoPE)
and a bidirectional gated-DeltaNet branch over the same normalized input. The
DeltaNet branch's Q/K/V and output projections are deleted and replaced by
references to the window branch's, so both mixers read the same linear
projections of $x$ (\texttt{liz2\_share\_qkv=False} restores independent
ones; \S\ref{sec:qkv-sharing}). The DeltaNet branch keeps its own value
expansion (factor 2), depthwise causal convolution, decay and write-strength
gates, output gate, and a merge of its forward and time-reversed passes
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:liz}). The two branch outputs are concatenated,
normalized with RMSNorm~\citep{zhang2019rmsnorm} and projected back to $d$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{LiZAttention2 forward (one layer)}
\label{alg:liz}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times L\times d}$; shared projections $W_q,W_k,W_v,W_o$
\State $o^{\text{swa}}\gets W_o\,\text{BidirWindowAttn}\big(\text{RoPE}(W_qx),\text{RoPE}(W_kx),W_vx;\,w\big)$
\For{$\text{dir}\in\{\text{fwd},\text{bwd}\}$}
  \State $x'\gets x$ if fwd, else $\text{flip}(x)$ \Comment{flip along the sequence axis}
  \State $(q,k,v)\gets\text{SiLU}\,\text{DepthwiseCausalConv}\big([W_qx';\,W_kx';\,E_vW_vx']\big)$ \Comment{$E_v$: value expansion}
  \State $\beta\gets\sigma(W_\beta x')$;\quad $g\gets-\exp(A_{\log})\,\text{softplus}(W_ax'+b_{dt})$ \Comment{per head}
  \State $o^{\text{dir}}\gets\text{GatedDeltaRule}(q,k,v;\,g,\beta)\odot\text{SiLU}(W_gx')$ \Comment{$q,k$ L2-normalized}
\EndFor
\State $o^{\Delta}\gets W_o\,W_{\text{lr}}\,\text{RMSNorm}\big([o^{\text{fwd}};\,\text{flip}(o^{\text{bwd}})]\big)$
\State \Return $W_{\text{mix}}\,\text{RMSNorm}\big([o^{\text{swa}};\,o^{\Delta}]\big)$ \Comment{$2d\to d$}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Block-AttnRes aggregation}
\label{sec:attnres}

Instead of the plain residual stream $x_{i+1}=x_i+f_i(x_i)$, each layer's
\emph{input} is a learned, softmax-weighted mix of the embedding, the
finalized block sums of earlier layers, and a running partial sum of the
current block~\citep{kimiteam2026attnres} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:attnres}); the
sublayers of $f_i$ still use ordinary residual connections around their own
input. The weight vector $w$ of each aggregator is initialized to zero, so the
initial mix is uniform. With block size $1$ each earlier layer output is a
separate source, a densely connected pattern~\citep{huang2016densenet} with learned
softmax weights; with block size $n_{\text{layers}}$ the sources reduce to the
embedding and one running sum, and larger sizes change nothing. The notebook config uses
block size $4$ over $4$ layers; the ablation networks have one layer, where
the aggregator is a structural no-op.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Block-AttnRes backbone forward}
\label{alg:attnres}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require embedding $x_0$; layers $f_1,\dots,f_L$; block size $S$; learned $w_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ per aggregator
\State $\text{completed}\gets[\,]$;\ $\text{partial}\gets\text{None}$;\ $\text{count}\gets0$
\For{$i=1,\dots,L$}
  \State $\text{sources}\gets[x_0]\cup\text{completed}\cup(\{\text{partial}\}\text{ if not None})$
  \State $V\gets\text{stack}(\text{sources})$;\quad $K\gets\text{RMSNorm}(V)$
  \State $h\gets\sum_{\text{sources}}\text{softmax}(w_i^{\top}K)\cdot V$ \Comment{softmax over sources}
  \State $x_i\gets f_i(h)$
  \State $\text{partial}\gets x_i$ if partial is None else $\text{partial}+x_i$;\quad $\text{count}\mathrel{+}=1$
  \If{$\text{count}=S$}
    \State $\text{completed}.\text{append}(\text{partial})$;\ $\text{partial}\gets\text{None}$;\ $\text{count}\gets0$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\text{RMSNorm}(x_L)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Sparse Mixture-of-Experts}
\label{sec:moe}

Each token is routed to $k$ of $E$ routed experts plus one or more always-on
shared experts. Balance is maintained by nudging a per-expert selection bias
toward equal load (DeepSeek-V3 style, no auxiliary loss by default). The
active-parameter count used throughout is computed directly:
\begin{equation}
N_{\text{act}}=N_{\text{total}}-N_{\text{expert}}+N_{\text{expert}}\frac{k}{E},
\label{eq:active}
\end{equation}
where $N_{\text{expert}}$ sums the parameters under modules whose name
contains \texttt{".experts."}; shared experts, the router, the attention and
DeltaNet path and the codec count as fully active
(\texttt{count\_active\_parameters}).

\subsection{KairosMemoryGate: cross-session recurrent-state memory}
\label{sec:memgate}

With \texttt{use\_memory\_gate=True}, the recurrent state of every DeltaNet
layer of every scale is seeded for a new sequence not from zero but from a
gate over a bank of states left by earlier sequences (the preceding batch's
final state, or an externally supplied bank). One gate module is shared by all
scales and layers. It is a single-head cross-attention over a rank-$r'$
bottleneck, $r'=\max(8,\text{round}(\sqrt{D}/4))$, where $D$ is the flattened
DeltaNet state size ($D=2048$ and $r'=11$ for the notebook config). The query is the
projection of the (zero) current state; the keys and values are that same
projection followed by the projected bank, so the gate can attend to the
bank or fall back on the current state (Algorithm~\ref{alg:memgate}). It is
applied to the forward pass only; the time-reversed pass starts from zero.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Memory gate, applied per (scale, DeltaNet layer)}
\label{alg:memgate}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require zero state $s_0\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times D}$; bank $M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times D}$ of flattened prior states
\If{$n=0$} \State \Return $s_0$ \EndIf
\If{$n=1$} \State \Return $M_1$ broadcast to $B$ rows \EndIf
\State $q\gets\text{Linear}_{D\to r'}(s_0)$;\quad $K\gets[\,q;\ \text{Linear}_{D\to r'}(M)\,]$
\State $o\gets\text{MultiheadAttention}_{h=1}(q,K,K)$
\State \Return $\text{Linear}_{r'\to D}(o)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Denoising objective and curriculum}
\label{sec:objective}

Given a clean byte sequence $x_0$, each row draws a rate
$p\sim U(\epsilon,p_{\max})$ and a Bernoulli$(p)$ mask over non-prompt,
non-padding positions. Masked positions are replaced by tokens drawn
uniformly from the vocabulary (and random octet-family ids), giving $x_t$; no
mask indicator is provided. The model predicts $x_0$ at the masked positions
with cross-entropy, weighted per token by $\min\!\big(c,\,1+\alpha(1/p-1)\big)$,
a blend between plain cross-entropy ($\alpha=0$) and full $1/p$ weighting
($\alpha=1$) in the manner of MDLM~\citep{sahoo2024mdlm}, clipped at $c$ to
bound the variance from rows with small $p$. With probability $0.5$ per batch,
self-conditioning~\citep{chen2022analogbits} feeds a detached, no-gradient
first prediction back in as an additive embedding, matching its use in
\texttt{generate()}. The default is $\epsilon=10^{-3}$, $c=3$; an
octet-family cross-entropy term with weight $1$ is added when family ids are
present.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Denoising training step (\texttt{compute\_loss})}
\label{alg:diffusion}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require batch $x_0$, prompt lengths, pad mask; schedule values $(p_{\max}^{(t)},\alpha^{(t)})$; clip $c$
\State $p_b\gets\epsilon+(p_{\max}^{(t)}-\epsilon)\,u_b$,\ $u_b\sim U(0,1)$ \Comment{one rate per row}
\State $m\gets\text{Bernoulli}(p_b)$ restricted to non-prompt, non-pad positions
\State $x_t\gets x_0$ with $x_t[m]\gets$ tokens drawn uniformly from the vocabulary
\If{$u<0.5$, $u\sim U(0,1)$} $\hat y\gets\text{stopgrad}(\text{model}(x_t))$; add $\text{softmax}(\hat y)E$ to the embeddings \EndIf
\State $\text{logits}\gets\text{model}(x_t,\ \text{logits\_mask}=m)$ \Comment{project masked positions only}
\State $\ell_i\gets\text{CE}(\text{logits}_i,x_{0,i})\cdot\min\big(c,\,1+\alpha^{(t)}(1/p_{b(i)}-1)\big)$
\State \Return $\text{mean}_i\,\ell_i$ \Comment{$+$ octet-family CE when family ids are present}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The schedule $(p_{\max}^{(t)},\alpha^{(t)})$ is a pure function of the global
step (\texttt{stage\_mask\_schedule}), so resuming from a checkpoint depends
only on the persisted step count. It is flat at $(0.3,\,0)$ in the MAE stage,
ramps linearly to $(1.0,\,1)$ over the transition stage, then stays flat in
the diffusion stage. The staging was introduced after an informal observation
of optimization instability when sampling the full masking range from a random
initialization; that observation is not quantified here. A seeded control without the
curriculum is reported in \S\ref{sec:curriculum-control}.

\subsection{Overfitting protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The purpose is a convergence check. A network that cannot drive the loss of a fixed
byte string to near zero has an optimization or implementation defect; success is necessary
and not sufficient for a useful model, since it says nothing about generalization.

All ablations use \texttt{kairos overfit} on the configurations of
\texttt{configs/ablations/} (\texttt{kairos==0.4.1.dev46},
\texttt{torch==2.14.0}), on CPU with eager attention and the PyTorch fallback
DeltaNet path. The data are 16 identical copies of one 440-byte string (the
sentence ``the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog'' repeated ten times),
truncated to 128 tokens, i.e.\ about $2.9$ repetitions per sequence; the
63-sentence control uses 63 distinct short sentences. Training uses batch
size 4, 600 steps unless noted, $p_{\max}=0.3$ and no reweighting; the
attention window is the library default $w=128$, which exceeds the 26
positions of the finest scale, so the window branch attends globally. Every
step draws a fresh corruption, and \emph{min.\ loss} is the minimum over the
run of the per-step training loss, an extreme value of a noisy per-batch
quantity. Training and evaluation data coincide, so the study measures
memorization speed, not generalization. The runs behind Tables~\ref{tab:seeds}--\ref{tab:qkv-sharing}
were not bit-reproducible at a fixed seed (the baseline at seed 0 gave $0.227$ in one run and $0.31$ in
another) because the seed only selected the training subset; it now also seeds initialization, step order and
masking, and the flow-matching runs of \S\ref{sec:flowarms} are reproducible. Seeds are 0, 1, 2 where stated; standard deviations are sample
standard deviations ($n-1$).

\subsection{Convergence by training objective: loss weight and corruption path}\label{sec:flowarms}

Beyond the architecture, the denoising objective is itself a factor of the convergence study. The uniform-noise
objective of \S\ref{sec:objective} is a special case of discrete flow matching~\citep{gat2024dfm}, so we vary the
corruption path and the loss weight with the architecture fixed. Because the loss definitions differ, this factor is
compared on a common reference rather than on the minimum training loss of the other tables.

Three arms share the tiny baseline model and train over the full range $p_{\max}=1$: the $1/p$-weighted objective of
\S\ref{sec:objective}, and two unweighted (discrete flow matching) arms with a linear or a cubic path. Training losses
differ in definition, so we compare on common measures taken before the weights are restored: the plain cross-entropy under
the linear path, and the exact-token rate of a 64-token continuation of a 32-token prompt, sampled from noise in 8 and 16
steps by the discrete flow-matching Euler sampler and by the entropy-bound sampler. Runs are seeded and reproducible, and
arms are paired by seed (four seeds).

\paragraph{Results at two learning rates.} Each experiment ran at a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, which was not swept, and
again at $8\times10^{-3}$, the rate selected for AdamW in \S\ref{sec:optarms}. Memorization uses 1800 steps on one sentence and
the unseen-passage test 1000 steps (48 training and 24 held-out passages); Table~\ref{tab:objective} gives the reference
cross-entropy on the memorized subset and on the held-out passages.

did not control, and we do not claim a cause.

We therefore report no advantage for flow matching in training or sampling at either rate, and a larger generalization
gap on unseen passages. The corrector, time conditioning, larger models and real corpora are untested (ablation log,
\S11).

\subsection{Convergence by training algorithm: optimizer and noise sampling}\label{sec:optarms}

Two further factors of the training algorithm are varied with the architecture and the objective of
\S\ref{sec:objective} fixed (full masking range, $1/p$ weight clipped at 10, linear path): the optimizer, and how the
noise level $t$ is drawn for the rows of a batch. The optimizer is AdamW; AdamW with betas $(0.9,0.95)$, weight decay $0.1$
and a 5\% linear warmup; or Muon~\citep{jordan2024muon} on the 2D matrices of the backbone (routers excluded) with AdamW on
every other parameter. The noise level is drawn independently per row (uniform, the default), one per stratum of width $1/B$
(stratified), or as $(t,1-t)$ pairs (antithetic). The antithetic arm pairs rows that carry different sequences, so it is not
the complementary-mask scheme of the literature, which pairs two corruptions of the same sequence.

\paragraph{Protocol.} The learning rate is swept per optimizer over seven values from $5\times10^{-4}$ to
$3.2\times10^{-2}$ (600 steps, seeds 9 and 10, which are not used afterwards) and the value with the lowest mean
reference cross-entropy is kept; all three optimizers select $8\times10^{-3}$, bracketed by $4\times10^{-3}$ and
$1.6\times10^{-2}$, and the two noise-sampling arms reuse the AdamW rate. The sweep is noisy over two seeds, for
instance AdamW scores $0.121$ at $3.2\times10^{-2}$ and $0.431$ at $1.6\times10^{-2}$. The final runs use the
1800-step memorization protocol of \S\ref{sec:flowarms} on seeds 0--3, paired by seed.

Table~\ref{tab:optarms} shows that no arm is separated from AdamW: every paired difference is smaller than its own standard deviation, and the seed spread

repeated 600-step runs. Three controls were run before accepting this as an

\paragraph{Seeded replication.} Five reproducible 600-step runs (seeds 0--4, otherwise the setup of
Table~\ref{tab:seeds}) give a minimum loss of $0.273 \pm 0.309$ for shared and $0.179 \pm 0.098$ for separate projections
(last-100 means $0.387$ and $0.272$; steps to a loss of 1: 232 and 221). Shared is lower on two seeds of five, so the
600-step row of Table~\ref{tab:qkv-sharing} is not reproduced. The earlier unseeded runs differed from the seeded ones
in an uncontrolled way (the seed then drove only the subset), and run-to-run noise of this size, one baseline seed at
$0.803$ against $0.07$--$0.29$, is enough to produce the apparent gap, though we did not isolate that cause.

The same check applies to \texttt{liz2} against \texttt{vanilla}: at
3\,000 steps \texttt{vanilla} reaches $0.0014$, matching \texttt{liz2}'s
$0.0011$. Computing steps-to-threshold from the saved curves shows that the
shared-versus-separate gap is concentrated in the $0.5$--$0.1$ loss range
($+9$ to $+14\%$ steps for separate projections) and vanishes elsewhere,
which a single comparison at a fixed step count would have hidden. A plausible
mechanism is that shared projections receive gradient from both branches at
every step, an effective higher early learning rate. The freezing control is
consistent with it but does not isolate it; a direct test would rescale the
learning rate of the separate variant. The lesson generalizes: a single-run
result on a tiny memorization task is a hypothesis until it survives a
step-budget control, a data-scale control and, where a mechanism is
suspected, a control that isolates it.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Summary of findings.} The tiny networks reach a near-zero loss on the
memorization task, so the implementation optimizes; this does not show that the model
learns transferable structure. Taken together, the observations suggest that:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the codec ranking of Table~\ref{tab:seeds} is stable across three seeds, but it was
        measured after 600 steps on one repeated sentence and is a memorization-speed ranking;
  \item the hybrid attention (\texttt{liz2}) is not separable from window attention alone at 600 steps
        over five seeded runs, and both reach the same floor at 3\,000 steps;
  \item the shared-projection advantage seen in unseeded runs does not replicate under five seeds
        (\S\ref{sec:qkv-sharing}); the earlier mechanism remained consistent with the freezing control but was never isolated;
  \item depth, top-1 versus top-2 routing and the AttnRes block size show no measurable
        effect in this setup, the block-size result being a saturation effect;
  \item the curriculum shows no detectable effect over four seeds on one memorized sentence
        (\S\ref{sec:curriculum-control}), and the memory gate has no supporting result beyond twelve
        optimizer steps.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Practical implications.} The ablation runs used a CPU. A memorization test of this
kind can be repeated whenever a component changes, but the seed spread ($\pm0.001$ to
$\pm0.309$ in Table~\ref{tab:seeds}) limits which differences it can detect.

\paragraph{Compute sizing of the target.}
\label{sec:sizing}
The 200M-total/25M-active design point has not been instantiated; a roofline
argument~\citep{williams2009roofline} indicates what a run would need. Training costs
$6N_{\text{act}}D=4.5\times10^{18}$ FLOPs for $D=30$B tokens~\citep{kaplan2020scaling}, a ratio
$D/N_{\text{act}}=1{,}200$ far below that of the dense Pythia-14M, trained on about 300B
tokens~\citep{biderman2023pythia}. Chinchilla is fitted on dense models of 70M parameters and
more~\citep{hoffmann2022chinchilla}, so applying it to 25M active parameters is an extrapolation,
and MoE scaling laws~\citep{ludziejewski2025moe,abnar2025parameters} parameterize loss rather
than step time. Under balanced routing, a microbatch of $T$ tokens touches nearly every expert
once $T$ exceeds a few times $E/k$, so a step reads $N_{\text{total}}$ weights, not
$N_{\text{act}}$; with bf16 weights the arithmetic intensity is
$\mathcal{I}(T)=(N_{\text{act}}/N_{\text{total}})\,T$ FLOP/byte, and the smallest microbatch that
reaches the ridge point $\mathcal{I}^{\ast}=\pi/b_s$ of a GPU with peak throughput $\pi$ and
bandwidth $b_s$ is
\begin{equation}
T_{\min}=\frac{N_{\text{total}}}{N_{\text{act}}}\,\mathcal{I}^{\ast}.
\label{eq:tmin}
\end{equation}

With the vendor figures of a T4, an A100 and an H100~\citep{nvidia2018t4,nvidia2020a100,nvidia2022h100}
and $N_{\text{total}}/N_{\text{act}}=8$, $T_{\min}$ is about 1.6k tokens (T4, A100) to 2.4k tokens
(H100), against 200--300 for a dense model of the same size; below it, throughput is set by
$N_{\text{total}}$, not by the active parameters that FLOP-centric accounting counts. Sparsity is
therefore not free on memory-limited hardware. These are upper bounds from a model that counts
weight traffic only and omits activations, DeltaNet states, codec convolutions, MoE dispatch and
communication, and any utilization would be assumed, not measured. Whether a 16~GB card holds a
2.4k-token microbatch of this architecture is an empirical question this report does not answer.
The numbers come from \texttt{scripts/roofline.py}.

\subsection{Limitations}

The ablation study memorizes one sentence in 16 copies on text only, with a window branch that
attends globally, on CPU, with the runs of Tables~\ref{tab:seeds}--\ref{tab:qkv-sharing} produced before seeds were fixed (not bit-reproducible); its metric is a minimum over
noisy per-step losses. Only Table~\ref{tab:seeds} and \S\ref{sec:qkv-sharing} carry multi-seed
or multi-control statistics; the other sweeps are single-run. Parameter counts of the ablation
rows in Table~\ref{tab:seeds} other than the baseline are carried over from earlier logs. The
notebook config's active-parameter count has not been logged from an instantiated model. No dense baseline
is compared, so nothing here supports a claim of superiority; the multimodal, curriculum and
memory-gate components have no supporting result yet. The target sizing is analytical.

\subsection{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

The long-term aim behind Kairos is a small multimodal foundation model that a single local
machine can pretrain end to end; this report only asks whether the substrate optimizes. The
next step toward that aim is a pretraining run, which would turn these sanity checks into
held-out results. The pretraining notebook (\texttt{notebook/kairos\_pretraining.py}) trains on the text corpus
\texttt{keep-it-simple}~\citep{keepitsimple2026} (640{,}646 rows of bidirectional
prompt--text pairs) and on \texttt{keep-it-simple-multimodal}~\citep{keepitsimplemm2026}
(6{,}336 rows, a deliberately small pilot mix), through the
MAE, transition and diffusion curriculum. Planned measurements: (i) held-out loss per curriculum
stage and per modality against trivial baselines, and text loss with and without the
multimodal data; (ii) a comparison with Pythia-14M~\citep{biderman2023pythia} in bits per byte,
since tokenizers and objectives differ. Equal total parameter counts do not mean equal compute:
Pythia's parameters are largely embeddings of a vocabulary of about 50k entries, whereas a
byte-level vocabulary puts nearly all Kairos parameters in the backbone, so total,
non-embedding and active parameters and FLOPs per byte should all be reported. A Pythia-shaped
model trained on the same data would separate architecture from data, and a held-out corpus
that neither model has seen would avoid favoring Kairos; (iii) 3-seed, steps-to-threshold
reruns of the single-run sweeps on diverse data; (iv) the curriculum control of \S\ref{sec:curriculum-control} repeated on held-out data and at larger scale; (v) a
measured tokens/s against microbatch size for the 200M/25M and a 50M/25M variant, which tests
Eq.~\ref{eq:tmin} directly.

\section{Conclusion}

Kairos poses a design question at 1--15M parameters: whether a hybrid bidirectional
DeltaNet/window-attention, MoE, denoising architecture over a byte-level space optimizes at
all. Memorization tests answer yes at this scale. The clearest architectural effect in the first runs, a
shared-projection advantage, closed with more steps or data and did not replicate across five seeded runs,
which is why we recommend seeded runs, steps-to-threshold and control runs before reading a tiny-scale comparison. Generalization,
multimodal training and comparison with existing models are the objectives of the run described
in \S\ref{sec:future}; the 200M/25M design point of \S\ref{sec:sizing} is a sizing exercise only.
